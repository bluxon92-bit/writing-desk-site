First person puts the reader inside one head from the opening line. Done well, it gives you the closest bond a story can offer between a reader and a character, carried almost entirely by voice. "Call me Ishmael", the opening of [*Moby-Dick*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2701), tells you who is talking before it tells you anything else, and you are already listening.

That closeness comes with rules of its own. This guide covers what first person point of view is, the types of first person narrator, when the choice pays off, how to build a voice and seven common mistakes with fixes you can apply in your next edit.

## What is first person point of view?

In first person point of view, a character in the story narrates it, using *I*, *me*, *my* and sometimes *we* and *our*. The reader sees only what that narrator sees, knows only what they know and meets every other character through the narrator's eyes.

**First person:** I pushed open the door and waited for the room to go quiet.
**Third person:** She pushed open the door and waited for the room to go quiet.

The events are the same. The difference is the relationship between narrator and reader. In first person, the narrator is a character with a voice, opinions and blind spots, and the reader is listening to them speak. For the wider picture, see our guide to [point of view](/blog/point-of-view/).

## What types of first person narrator are there?

**The protagonist narrator.** The main character tells their own story. Katniss Everdeen in *The Hunger Games* and Holden Caulfield in *The Catcher in the Rye* work this way, and so does Ryland Grace in Andy Weir's *Project Hail Mary*.

**The peripheral narrator.** A witness tells the story of someone else. Nick Carraway narrates *The Great Gatsby*, and Dr Watson narrates most of the Sherlock Holmes stories. A peripheral narrator lets you present a larger-than-life central character through an observer the reader trusts.

**The retrospective narrator.** An older narrator looks back at events. Charlotte Brontë's [*Jane Eyre*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1260) has Jane tell her story from years afterwards, and Scout Finch narrates *To Kill a Mockingbird* as an adult recalling her childhood. The narrator knows how it ends and can shape the telling.

**The present-tense narrator.** The narrator tells the story as it happens. *The Hunger Games* is told in present tense, which gives it immediacy and means the narrator cannot hint at what comes next.

**Multiple first person narrators.** Several characters take turns. *Gone Girl* alternates between Nick and Amy, and each voice gives the reader a different version of the same marriage. A change of narrator sits naturally at a chapter break, and our guide to [words per chapter](/blog/words-per-chapter/) covers planning them.

**First person plural.** A group narrates as *we*. It is rare, and Jeffrey Eugenides uses it in *The Virgin Suicides*, where the neighbourhood boys tell the story together.

## When does first person work best?

First person suits stories built on one person's inner life. Common homes for it include:

- **Coming-of-age and young adult fiction.** The voice of a young narrator working out who they are gives these stories their energy. *The Catcher in the Rye* and *The Hunger Games* both show it.
- **Crime and noir.** A wry or weary narrator's take on events becomes part of the pleasure.
- **Survival and science fiction.** Andy Weir's *The Martian* is told through the log entries of an astronaut stranded on Mars, and the narrator's humour carries the reader through the technical problems.
- **Memoir and confessional fiction.** The form fits stories of personal experience.
- **Unreliable narrator stories.** First person is the natural home of the [unreliable narrator](/blog/unreliable-narrator/). In Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day*, the butler Stevens tells his story in a formal, restrained voice, and the gaps in what he says tell the reader more than his words do.

Fantasy writers can use it too. Robin Hobb's *Assassin's Apprentice* is narrated by Fitz in first person, looking back on his life as a royal bastard and assassin.

## What are the pros and cons of first person?

| | Strengths | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Intimacy | Direct access to the narrator's thoughts, feelings and fears | A long novel can tire the reader if the voice is thin |
| Voice | Personality carries the story from the first line | Everything is filtered through one narrator's biases |
| Scope | Focused and immediate | The reader only learns what the narrator can see, hear or be told |
| Suspense | The reader learns things as the narrator does | The narrator must be present for every key event |
| Unreliability | Built in, if you want it | Needs careful planting so the reader can spot the gaps |

## Should you use past or present tense in first person?

Past tense gives you a narrator who has already lived the story, which lets you use two perspectives at once: the experiencing self who goes through the events and the narrating self who tells them. Jane Eyre's famous "Reader, I married him" is the narrating self speaking directly. That layer lets a retrospective narrator foreshadow, reflect and judge their younger self.

![Timeline of a retrospective narrator: the experiencing self lives the events, and the narrating self tells the story years later](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnkddcxrt/image/upload/v1794780648/retrospective-narrator-two-selves_rptp2m.svg)

Present tense removes hindsight. The narrator knows only what they know in this moment, and the reader waits in the same uncertainty. That works well for fast, tense stories, and it limits the narrator's ability to reflect.

Pick one, and keep it consistent. If you use a retrospective narrator, decide how much the narrating self knows and keep that level steady across the book.

## What are the common mistakes in first person, and how do you fix them?

### 1. Filter words between the narrator and the moment

*I saw*, *I felt*, *I heard* and *I noticed* put a layer between the reader and the scene, and first person is already close. The narrator's experience is the story, so say what happens.

**Filtered:** I felt my stomach drop as I saw the car pull into the drive.
**Direct:** The car pulled into the drive. My stomach dropped.

Our guide to [filter words](/blog/filter-words/) has the full list and the exceptions worth keeping.

### 2. Starting every sentence with "I"

A run of sentences that all begin with *I* reads like a list of what the narrator did.

**Repetitive:** I walked to the shop. I bought milk. I saw Tom. I said hello.
**Varied:** The shop had run out of the good milk. Tom was in the queue ahead of me, and I said hello before he could pretend not to see me.

Keep the pronoun where it belongs, and vary what leads each sentence so the narrator's observations set the rhythm instead of a list of their actions.

### 3. The narrator knows what they cannot know

First person gives you a hard rule: the narrator can only report what they perceive, remember or learn. A slip breaks the spell.

**Slip:** Behind the door, Maya was hiding the letter from her father.
**Fixed:** Maya came out of the room with her hands behind her back, her face too bright.

When you need information the narrator lacks, let another character tell them, let them overhear it or let them find a document. Peripheral narrators, letters, diary entries and a second narrator are all ways to widen what the reader can see.

### 4. Every character sounds like the narrator

Because the narrator's voice dominates the page, it tends to leak into dialogue. Cover the names in a conversation and check whether you can tell who is speaking. If not, give each character their own vocabulary, rhythm and habits. Our guide to [writing realistic dialogue](/blog/how-to-write-realistic-dialogue/) shows how.

### 5. Thought stalls the action

First person invites interior monologue, and a paragraph of reflection in the middle of a tense scene slows it to a stop. Keep thoughts short when [pace](/blog/pacing/) matters, and place longer reflection in quieter moments. Showing the narrator's feelings through what they do keeps the scene moving, as in [show, don't tell](/blog/show-dont-tell/).

### 6. Describing the narrator in a mirror

A narrator who studies their reflection to list their hair and eye colour has stopped telling the story to hand over information. Use other characters' reactions, a remark from a relative or a small detail that comes up in action.

**Mirror:** I looked at my reflection. Brown hair, green eyes, a crooked nose.
**Reaction:** "You've got your mother's nose," Gran said, as she did every Sunday, and tapped it with a finger that smelled of tobacco.

### 7. Hindsight that comes and goes

A retrospective narrator who hints at the future in chapter one but forgets what they know in chapter nine confuses the reader. Decide what the narrating self knows and apply it evenly. Tense slips belong here too, since a narrator who drifts between past and present loses the reader's trust.

## How do you build a distinctive first person voice?

Voice comes from three things: what the narrator notices, how their sentences move and what they avoid saying. Two narrators in the same kitchen show the difference.

**Narrator A, precise and wary:** Four chairs, three of them matching. I counted them twice, the way I count everything when a room goes quiet.

**Narrator B, restless and funny:** Somebody had left a whole cake on the table, which was either a trap or the best news I'd had all week.

Same room, different people. Build a voice by deciding the narrator's:

- **Vocabulary.** The words they reach for and the words they never use.
- **Rhythm.** Long, looping sentences or short, clipped ones.
- **Preoccupations.** The things they notice first, which reveal what they fear or want and often trace back to their [backstory](/blog/backstory/).
- **Blind spots.** What they misread about themselves or others, which is where an unreliable narrator comes from.

A voice can also change on purpose. In Daniel Keyes's *Flowers for Algernon*, the narrator writes progress reports, and the spelling, vocabulary and sentence structure shift as his intelligence changes. The voice carries the story.

## How do you handle what the narrator cannot see?

Every first person writer faces the same problem: events happen elsewhere. These techniques keep the narrator's limits intact:

- **Let other characters report.** A friend arrives with news, or an overheard conversation fills a gap. Deliver it inside a scene so it lands as story, and see our guide to [exposition](/blog/what-is-exposition/) for how.
- **Use documents.** Letters, diaries, logs, emails and recordings let the narrator, and the reader, learn what happened out of sight. *The Martian* is built on logs.
- **Add a second narrator.** *Gone Girl* gives the reader a second vantage point by alternating voices.
- **Choose a peripheral narrator.** A witness close to the action can see more of the central character's life.
- **Accept the gaps.** What the narrator cannot know can fuel suspense, since the reader waits to learn it alongside them.

## Should you write your novel in first person?

Ask these questions before you commit:

1. Is the story centred on one person's inner life and voice?
2. Can that voice sustain a full-length novel without wearing the reader out?
3. Can every key event happen where the narrator can see it, or reach them by another route?
4. Do you want the reader to share the narrator's blind spots?

If the answers are mostly yes, first person is a strong fit. If you want to range across many characters and locations, look at third person instead, and see [third person limited vs omniscient](/blog/third-person-limited-vs-omniscient/) for how to choose between them.

Before you decide, write the same scene both ways. A few hundred words in first person and a few hundred in third will show you quickly which one feels natural.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is first person point of view?

First person point of view is narration by a character in the story, using *I*, *me* and *my*. The reader sees only what the narrator sees and knows only what they know.

### What are examples of first person narration?

*The Hunger Games*, *The Catcher in the Rye*, *Jane Eyre*, *To Kill a Mockingbird*, *The Great Gatsby*, *Gone Girl*, *The Martian* and *The Remains of the Day* are all told in first person.

### What are the pros and cons of first person?

First person offers intimacy, a strong voice and built-in suspense. It limits the story to what the narrator can perceive, and a long novel needs a voice with enough range to hold the reader.

### Can you write fantasy in first person?

Yes. Robin Hobb's *Assassin's Apprentice* is told in first person by Fitz, and the form lets the reader experience a fantasy world through one narrator's limited understanding of it.

### What is the difference between first person and third person limited?

Both stay with one character's perspective. First person uses *I* and gives the narrator a voice. Third person limited uses *he*, *she* or *they* and keeps a narrator one step back. See [third person limited vs omniscient](/blog/third-person-limited-vs-omniscient/).

### What tense should first person be in?

Past tense suits a narrator looking back and allows reflection and foreshadowing. Present tense gives immediacy and removes hindsight. Choose one and keep to it.

## Final words

First person rewards a writer who knows their narrator well: the vocabulary, the rhythm, what they notice and what they miss. Fix the seven mistakes above in your next [line pass](/blog/self-edit-novel/) and the voice has room to do its work.
