Every memorable character has a constellation of traits, qualities that shape how they think, speak, act, and react. But "good character traits" is vague advice. The real work is knowing which traits to choose, how to make them specific, and how to let them create friction.

Here's a working master list, plus the craft principles that turn a list of adjectives into an actual person.

## What Are Character Traits?

Character traits are the consistent qualities that define how a character behaves across different situations. They're not the same as mood, which is temporary, or circumstance, which is external. Traits are stable. They're the patterns of behaviour and perception that emerge regardless of what's happening around the character.

A character who is stubborn will be stubborn whether they're at home or in battle. A character who is suspicious will find things to be suspicious about in every setting. Traits drive behaviour, which drives plot.

## How Many Traits Does a Character Need?

Enough to feel real. Too many to feel like a checklist.

In practice, most well-drawn characters have two or three defining traits, the ones that drive their major decisions, and a handful of secondary traits that add texture.

The defining traits are the ones that create conflict. They're the qualities that put your character in friction with the world, with other characters, or with themselves. A generous character in a world that rewards selfishness. An honest one in a world built on deception.

## Positive Character Traits

These traits tend to generate sympathy and drive proactive behaviour. The most useful ones for fiction are those that carry a cost or a complication, that create as many problems as they solve.

Brave, Willing to act in the face of fear. Can tip into recklessness. Loyal, Committed to relationships and people. Can become blind devotion. Empathetic, Attuned to others' feelings. Can become a liability in high-stakes situations. Curious, Driven to understand. Can override caution. Generous, Gives freely. Can be exploited. Patient, Willing to wait. Can enable situations that need confrontation. Principled, Guided by values. Can become rigid and self-righteous. Adaptable, Changes with circumstances. Can lack conviction. Determined, Keeps going. Can refuse to accept when stopping is the right call. Perceptive, Reads people and situations accurately. Knows things others don't, which can be isolating. Compassionate, Moved by others' suffering. Can paralyse decision-making. Creative, Finds unconventional solutions. Can struggle within systems or structures. Honest, Tells the truth. Can be brutal when kindness is needed. Calm, Doesn't escalate. Can be mistaken for indifference. Protective, Shields others. Can become controlling.

## Negative Character Traits

These traits generate conflict and complication. Used thoughtfully, they make characters feel real, because real people have qualities that hurt themselves and others.

Arrogant, Overestimates their own abilities or importance. Often can't hear good advice. Cowardly, Avoids necessary confrontation. Their avoidance often creates worse problems. Manipulative, Uses others for their own ends. Can be compelling as a POV character. Jealous, Threatened by others' success or relationships. Drives some of fiction's most dramatic conflicts. Cruel, Causes harm, whether casually or deliberately. The specifics matter, what kind of cruelty, toward whom. Selfish, Prioritises their own interests at others' expense. Works best when the character can see what they're doing and does it anyway. Impulsive, Acts before thinking. Creates consequences that drive plot. Dishonest, Lies to others or themselves. Excellent for [unreliable narrator](/blog/unreliable-narrator/)s. Closed-minded, Refuses to update beliefs when confronted with evidence. Can be tragic. Vindictive, Needs revenge. Often cycles back into harm. Passive, Lets things happen rather than acting. Can be interesting as a character study; challenging to make them dynamic. Envious, Wants what others have. The sibling of jealousy but distinct, envy is about lacking, jealousy about losing. Paranoid, Sees threat everywhere. Can make them right, which is interesting territory. Vain, Obsessed with appearance or reputation. Drives choices that make no rational sense. Reckless, Takes unnecessary risks. Often charming until the consequences land.

## Neutral Traits (Which Become Interesting in Context)

These traits aren't inherently positive or negative, they become meaningful based on what they're in friction with.

Reserved, Doesn't share much. In a context requiring trust-building, this is a liability. In one requiring discretion, an asset. Ambitious, Wants more. Whether this is positive or negative depends entirely on what they're willing to do for it. Pragmatic, Focused on what works. Can cut through noise; can also cut through humanity. Traditional, Respects the established way. Positive in a story about preservation; a liability in one about necessary change. Intense, Fully committed to everything. Exhausting to others; impressive in crisis. Analytical, Processes emotionally by thinking. Misread constantly in interpersonal situations. Independent, Doesn't need others. Creates interesting problems in ensemble casts. Nostalgic, Lives partly in the past. Can be moving; can be a way of avoiding the present. Competitive, Has to win. Functional in some contexts, corrosive in others.

## Making Traits Work Together: Contradiction and Complexity

The most compelling characters aren't just one trait. They're combinations that surprise, traits that shouldn't coexist but do, because humans are contradictory.

Brave and cowardly in different contexts. A character who faces physical danger without flinching but cannot have an honest conversation with someone they love.

Kind but dishonest. Someone whose lies are motivated by genuine care for others. This is a perfect internal conflict, their virtue and their flaw are the same thing.

Ambitious but self-sabotaging. The character who wants success but does things that undermine it. Often rooted in deep belief that they don't deserve it.

Loyal to the wrong people. Loyalty is generally positive. Directed at someone who exploits it, it becomes the engine of tragedy.

When you're building a character, don't just pick a list of adjectives. Ask: how do these traits sit together? Where do they create internal friction? Where does one trait undermine another?

## Character Traits vs. Character Flaws

Traits and flaws often overlap, but they're not the same thing. A flaw is a specific quality that creates problems, for the character, for others, or for the story. Traits are broader.

Every meaningful character should have at least one flaw that drives the story's conflict. That flaw should be connected to, often the shadow side of, one of their defining positive traits. See the article on [character flaws](/blog/character-flaws/) for a more detailed breakdown.

## Using Character Traits in Writing Desk

> **Writing Desk tip:** The character notes feature is built exactly for this kind of work. Capture a character's defining traits, track how those traits are demonstrated across scenes, and check whether they're behaving consistently or starting to drift.

Consistency doesn't mean characters should be predictable. It means that when they do something unexpected, it should feel like something they could do, given everything you know about them. Having their traits documented makes that check much easier.

## A Final Note: Show, Don't State

The list above is a reference, not a recipe. In your actual prose, character traits should never be stated directly, or almost never. Don't tell us your protagonist is brave. Put them in a situation that requires courage and show what they do.

Readers form impressions of character through behaviour, dialogue, decision-making, and the gap between what a character says and what they do. That's where traits live, not in description, but in action.
