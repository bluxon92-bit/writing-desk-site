I don't like the phrase "write faster." It implies the problem is speed, which it usually isn't.

Most novelists who feel slow aren't slow, they're inefficient in specific, fixable ways. They spend twenty minutes finding where they left off. They edit the sentence they just wrote before writing the next one. They sit down to write when their mind is already spent. They have a vague target they never quite hit and no real way to know whether today was a good session or a bad one.

Fix those things and you don't write faster, you write more, which isn't quite the same thing, but it's what you actually need.

Here's what's actually helped me.

---

## Know Your Numbers

The writers I know who finish novels consistently are the ones who track their output. Not obsessively, but they know roughly how many words they write in a good session, what their average is over a week, and how far they are from the end.

This matters because "I'll write more" is not a plan. "I'll write 500 words every weekday and I'm currently 18,000 words into an 80,000-word novel" is a plan with a finish date.

Pick a daily target that's achievable on your worst day, not your best. 300–500 words sounds low. It's 35,000–50,000 words over six months. That's a first draft.

---

## Protect Your Best Hours

Most people have a peak cognitive window, two to four hours where they do their best thinking. For most people it's in the morning. For some it's late at night. Wherever it falls for you: don't use it for email.

Writing fiction is the hardest cognitive task you do. It deserves your sharpest hours. If you're writing after a full day of other work and wondering why the scenes feel flat, this might be part of the reason.

I write between 6am and 8am, before anything else happens to my attention. Not every day. But as close to every day as I can manage. Those two hours produce more usable words than the five I might scrape together across an afternoon and evening.

---

## Stop Editing as You Draft

This is the single most damaging habit for word count, and I still catch myself doing it.

Writing and editing are different cognitive modes. When you write, you're generating. When you edit, you're evaluating. Switching between them constantly means you're never fully in either one. The result is slow, self-conscious prose that often isn't even better, just more laboured.

Write the sentence. Write the next sentence. Fix them tomorrow. The permission to write badly is the thing that keeps the draft moving. You can't revise a blank page, but you can revise a terrible first draft.

If you physically can't stop editing, if your eyes are drawn back to the last paragraph before you can write the next one, try turning off your screen while you draft. It works.

---

## End Sessions Mid-Sentence

Hemingway's advice and still the most useful productivity trick I know. Don't stop at the end of a chapter or a scene, stop in the middle of something, somewhere you know what comes next. Leave the ending of the sentence or paragraph unfinished in your document.

When you sit down for the next session, you're not starting from nothing. You're finishing a sentence. The momentum already exists; you just have to pick it up. Those first five minutes of a session, staring at the blank space after your last scene, are where more time gets lost than anywhere else.

---

## Reduce Friction at the Start of Every Session

How long does it take you to find where you left off? To open the right file, the right chapter, the right notes? Every minute of that warm-up is a minute you didn't write.

Keep your draft open. Leave your notes visible. Write a sentence at the end of each session about what you were doing and what comes next, two lines, not a full summary. Make it trivial to sit down and start.

---

## Use Writing Sprints

A sprint is a fixed period of focused writing with no interruptions. Twenty-five minutes is the classic (the Pomodoro technique). Thirty minutes, or fifty, if you can sustain it.

The constraint is the point. A sprint with a timer running produces more words than the same amount of open-ended writing time, because the boundary removes the option of drifting. You write until the timer stops.

Some writers do this with others, online sprint communities, write-ins, accountability partners, because the social element adds another layer of commitment. If that helps you, use it.

---

## Track Your Stats

You don't have to track every metric. But knowing your daily word count, your weekly total, and your average is low-effort accountability that works.

When I started keeping records, two things happened. The good writing days became more frequent, because I was actively trying to replicate them. The bad ones became less demoralising, because I could see the overall trend even on a zero-word day.

You also learn things about your own patterns that you can't know without data. Which days of the week are your best. Whether writing in the evening after a drink actually produces more or less than you think. What a realistic weekly average actually looks like, versus what you tell yourself.

---

## The Real Thing

None of this matters if you're not writing regularly. The tricks and techniques and productivity frameworks are all downstream of one thing: showing up at the same time, in the same place, with a target you can actually hit, and doing it again the next day.

Speed comes from consistency, not from sitting down with more urgency. The writers who finish novels aren't faster, they're more consistent. That's the thing worth optimising for.
