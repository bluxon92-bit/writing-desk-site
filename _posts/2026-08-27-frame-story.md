A frame story is a narrative structure in which one story contains another: an outer story that establishes a narrator or a situation, within which the main story is told. The outer layer is the frame. The story being told inside it is what most readers experience as the actual novel.

Frame stories are old. *The Canterbury Tales* frames its individual stories within a pilgrimage; *Frankenstein* frames Victor Frankenstein's account within Robert Walton's letters home; *Wuthering Heights* frames the central story within Lockwood's secondhand account, relayed to him by the housekeeper Nelly Dean.

## Why Writers Use a Frame

**It creates distance and perspective.** A frame lets the reader know from the outset how the inner story ends, or at least that it ends, since someone is alive to be telling it. This can build a particular kind of dread or inevitability that a story told in the moment can't achieve in the same way.

**It adds a layer of unreliability.** Once a story is being retold by a narrator within the frame, rather than experienced directly, questions about memory, bias, and [unreliable narration](/blog/unreliable-narrator/) enter naturally. The frame narrator's own perspective shapes how the inner story is told, whether or not that's made explicit.

**It provides thematic commentary.** The frame and the inner story can comment on each other. A frame narrator's reactions to the story they're relaying, or the circumstances under which they're telling it, can reframe how the reader interprets events in the inner story.

**It justifies structure that would otherwise feel unmotivated.** A story told out of chronological order, or through multiple secondhand accounts, can feel arbitrary without a frame. A frame gives the reader a reason the story is being told this particular way.

## Risks Worth Knowing

**The frame can feel like a detour.** If the outer story isn't compelling in its own right, readers may treat it as an obstacle between them and the story they actually want, particularly if the frame reappears periodically and interrupts the inner story's momentum.

**A weak or unresolved frame undermines the whole structure.** If a frame is set up but never meaningfully returned to or resolved, readers can feel the device was cosmetic rather than load-bearing.

**Double distance can weaken emotional stakes.** A story told by a narrator, about events told to them by someone else, is two removes from the reader. That distance is sometimes exactly the effect a writer wants; other times it quietly saps tension from scenes that would land harder told directly.

## When a Frame Story Earns Its Place

A frame works best when it's doing something the story couldn't do without it: creating dramatic irony through hindsight, justifying an unconventional structure, adding a layer of interpretation the reader needs to actively work through. If the frame could be removed entirely with no loss to the reader's experience of the inner story, it's worth asking what it's actually contributing.

## Final Words

A frame story is a structural choice with real cost, an extra layer between the reader and the events that matter most, and it should be made deliberately, for a reason specific to what that distance and perspective adds. Used well, it turns a straightforward story into one about the act of telling it. Used without a clear purpose, it's simply an obstacle between the reader and the book they picked up.
