The big frameworks (three-act, Save the Cat, the [Hero's Journey](/blog/heros-journey/)) describe the shape of a whole novel. Scene and sequel describes something smaller and, in a lot of ways, more useful day to day: the shape of a single unit of story, repeated hundreds of times across a manuscript.

If your novel-level structure is sound but individual chapters still feel rushed, aimless, or emotionally flat, the problem is usually happening at this level, not the big one.

## Where It Comes From

The scene/sequel model comes from Dwight Swain's *Techniques of the Selling Writer* (1965), one of the more technical and mechanically precise craft books ever written for genre fiction. Swain's core observation: readers don't just want to see a character act, they want to see the character process what happened before acting again. A story made entirely of action, with no processing, reads like a list of events rather than an experience.

## What a Scene Is

A scene is a unit built around a character with a clear goal, encountering conflict, and ending in a way that changes their situation for the worse. Swain broke it into three parts:

**Goal.** What does the point-of-view character want in this specific unit of story? Not their overall novel-length goal, their goal for this scene: get the file, win the argument, survive the crossing.

**Conflict.** Something opposes the goal. Without opposition, the goal is simply achieved and the scene has no tension.

**Disaster.** The scene ends with the character failing to get the goal, getting it in a way that creates a new problem, or getting it at a cost that outweighs the win. A scene that ends cleanly, with the goal simply achieved and nothing complicating it, tends to deflate tension rather than build it.

## What a Sequel Is

A sequel is the unit that follows a scene, and it's the part most first drafts skip or rush through. It's where the character processes the disaster before acting again. Swain gave it three parts too:

**Reaction.** The character's emotional response to what just happened. This can be brief, a paragraph, a few lines of interiority, but skipping it entirely makes characters feel like they absorb disaster with no cost.

**Dilemma.** The character weighs their options. Crucially, none of the options should be good. If there's an obviously correct choice, there's no dilemma and no tension.

**Decision.** The character chooses a course of action, which becomes the goal of the next scene. This is what keeps the story moving forward rather than just reacting indefinitely.

## Why the Pairing Matters

Scene and sequel alternate. Scene, sequel, scene, sequel, chapter after chapter. Skip too many sequels and readers stop believing your characters have an inner life; everything becomes plot happening at them with no processing in between. Skip too many scenes and let sequels run long, and the [pacing](/blog/pacing/) collapses; nothing is actually happening, just reflection.

Most pacing problems trace back to one of these two failures. A chapter that feels rushed usually has scene with no sequel: event after event with no room to land. A chapter that drags usually has sequel with no scene: reflection and interiority with no active conflict driving it forward.

## Using It to Diagnose a Draft

This model is most useful as a diagnostic tool during revision, not as something you plan scene-by-scene while drafting. Go through a chapter that isn't working and ask:

- Does the point-of-view character have a clear goal in this unit, or is the scene just something happening around them?
- Is there real conflict standing between them and that goal, or do they get what they want too easily?
- Does the scene end with their situation genuinely worse, or does it just... end?
- After a major disaster, do we see the character react and decide, or do they simply show up in the next scene already knowing what to do?

A chapter that fails all four questions usually isn't a structure problem in the big-picture sense. It's a scene/sequel problem, and it's fixable at the paragraph level rather than requiring a rewrite of your outline.

## Final Words

Scene and sequel won't tell you what your novel is about, or where your midpoint should land, or how your act breaks should work. What it does is give every chapter the same internal logic: want something, run into trouble, feel the cost, decide what's next. Novels that feel propulsive and novels that feel like a slog are often built from the same big-picture structure. The difference is usually happening here, at the level of the individual scene.
