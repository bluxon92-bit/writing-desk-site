Mr Collins in [*Pride and Prejudice*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1342) is built almost entirely from one quality: pompous deference. Elizabeth Bennet, in the same novel, holds contradictions, misjudges the people she thinks she reads best and changes her mind. Both characters work, because Jane Austen knew what each one was for.

That is the idea behind round and flat characters. A cast needs different amounts of depth, and the craft lies in giving each character the depth the story asks of them. This guide covers the definitions, a set of tests you can run on any character, when a flat character is the right choice and how to round one out when it needs more.

## What is a flat character?

A flat character is built around a single idea or quality. The novelist E.M. Forster named the distinction in his 1927 book [*Aspects of the Novel*](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/e-m-forster/aspects-of-the-novel/text/chapter-4), where he notes that flat characters were called "humours" in the seventeenth century and are sometimes called types or caricatures. His practical definition is that you can sum one up in a single sentence.

His main example is Mrs Micawber in Dickens's [*David Copperfield*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/766). Her whole self is a vow, "I never will desert Mr Micawber", and every time she appears she keeps it. You are never surprised by her, and you recognise her at once.

You will also see a flat character called a one-dimensional or two-dimensional character. These labels describe the same thing: a character with little complexity, few goals of their own and little or no growth.

## What is a round character?

A round character has several traits, some of them in tension, and an inner life the reader can feel. They want things, they contradict themselves, and under pressure they can do something you did not see coming.

Forster gives a simple test. A round character can surprise the reader in a convincing way. A character who never surprises is flat. A character who surprises without convincing is a flat character pretending to be round, because the surprise came from the author's needs and not from the person on the page. The same logic governs a [plot twist](/blog/plot-twist/): a surprise lands when the reader can look back and see it was earned.

Forster names Becky Sharp in *Vanity Fair*, Madame Bovary and the principal characters of Dostoevsky among his round characters, and says Jane Austen's characters are round or capable of roundness. Elizabeth Bennet shows why. She is certain she judges people accurately, then discovers she has misjudged both Darcy and Wickham. The surprise lands because her pride and her quick judgement were visible all along.

## Round vs flat characters: the key differences

| | Flat character | Round character |
|---|---|---|
| Built around | One idea or quality | Several traits, some in tension |
| Summed up in | One sentence | Hard to reduce to a sentence |
| Surprises the reader | Rarely | Yes, and the surprise convinces |
| Changes over the story | Little or not at all | Can change, or resist change under pressure |
| Reader's experience | Recognised instantly each time they return | Understood better as the story goes on |
| Typical role | Minor, comic or supporting | Protagonist, antagonist and major allies |

## When does a flat character work?

Flat characters do real work for a story, and Forster lists why:

- **Readers recognise them the moment they return.** There is no need to reintroduce them or watch for development.
- **They stay inside your plan.** A flat character will not run away with a scene or pull the story somewhere you did not intend.
- **They bring their own atmosphere.** One strong quality can set a tone in a line or two.
- **They are easy to remember.** A character who never changes sticks in the memory.

Forster adds that flat characters work best when they are comic, and that a serious flat character tends to bore. Mr Collins is a good case: his single note is funny every time it sounds.

Flat characters suit plot-driven stories such as mysteries and thrillers, where the reader's attention belongs on the puzzle. They also suit the supporting roles that surround a complex protagonist: the gatekeeper, the henchman, the comic neighbour, the [foil](/blog/antagonist-foil-character/) who exists to show the hero's qualities by contrast. Rounding every minor character costs reader attention and can pull focus from the people who carry the story. A [subplot](/blog/what-is-a-subplot/) is a natural place to give a supporting character a little extra depth.

## Are flat and round characters the same as static and dynamic?

Two different scales are in play. Flat and round describe depth: how complex a character is. [Static and dynamic](/blog/static-vs-dynamic-characters/) describe change: whether a character grows over the story.

The scales overlap. A flat character, built from one quality, has little to change, so flat characters tend to be static. A round character can go either way. One may transform across the book, and another may hold firm through everything the plot throws at them and finish unchanged, with all their depth intact.

## How can you tell if a character is flat?

Run these four tests on any character you suspect of being thin.

1. **The one-sentence test.** Can you describe them completely in a single sentence? If yes, they are flat. That is fine for a bit part and a warning sign for a major character.
2. **The surprise test.** Find a moment where they do something unexpected that the reader still believes. If you cannot find one, there is no depth to draw on yet.
3. **The off-page test.** What does the character want when the scene ends and they walk out of the room? If you have no answer, they exist only to serve the scene.
4. **The cover-the-names test.** Hide the names in a page of dialogue. If you cannot tell who is speaking, the voices have not separated, and the characters are likely sharing a thin set of traits. Our guide to [writing realistic dialogue](/blog/how-to-write-realistic-dialogue/) shows how to separate voices.

## How do you turn a flat character into a round one?

Work through these in order. Each step adds a layer, and a minor character may only need the first or second.

1. **Give them a want of their own.** A character with a goal that sometimes clashes with the protagonist's has a life beyond the plot. Our guide to [character motivation](/blog/character-motivation/) shows how to build one.
2. **Add a flaw that costs them something.** A [character flaw](/blog/character-flaws/) creates friction between who they are and what they need.
3. **Pair traits that pull against each other.** Roundness comes from contradiction: a generous person who cannot forgive, a fearless person who is afraid of being known. Browse our list of [character traits](/blog/character-traits/) for combinations.
4. **Show them through specific detail.** A habit, a phrase or a small gesture does more than an adjective. [Indirect characterisation](/blog/indirect-characterisation/) and [character mannerisms](/blog/character-mannerisms/) cover the techniques, and [show, don't tell](/blog/show-dont-tell/) applies to every detail you choose.
5. **Put them under pressure.** Depth appears when a character's formula stops working. Give them a scene where the one thing they always do is the wrong move. Our guide to [creating tension](/blog/how-to-create-tension/) covers how to build that pressure.
6. **Build a profile and a backstory.** Even [backstory](/blog/backstory/) details that never reach the page change how you write them. Start with a [character profile](/blog/how-to-create-a-character-profile/), try the [character development exercises](/blog/character-development-exercises/) or spark ideas with the [character backstory generator](/tools/character-backstory-generator/).
7. **Map the arc, if they are a protagonist.** A round main character usually needs a [character arc](/blog/character-arc/) that shows what they believe at the start and what the story costs them.

For example, take a night guard whose only trait is rigidity. Give him a daughter he is saving to send to a school he cannot afford, and a rule he has never once broken. Now one scene, where a stranger offers money to look the other way, tests both. He is the same guard, with a reason behind the rigidity and a moment where it could crack.

## How do you keep flat characters useful?

A flat character stays useful when you sharpen the one quality and keep it consistent. Give them a vivid tag, a distinct voice and a short stay on the page. Resist adding backstory they will never use.

Protagonists need more. A flat main character has nowhere to go, and Forster's warning about serious flat characters applies in full. If your lead keeps failing the one-sentence test, return to the steps above.

## Examples of flat and round characters in fiction

| Character | Book | Flat or round | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mrs Micawber | *David Copperfield* | Flat | Defined by her vow never to desert Mr Micawber |
| Mr Collins | *Pride and Prejudice* | Flat | Built from pompous deference, and comic because it never varies |
| Becky Sharp | *Vanity Fair* | Round | Forster's example of a character who convinces and surprises |
| Madame Bovary | *Madame Bovary* | Round | Forster counts her among the round characters |
| Elizabeth Bennet | *Pride and Prejudice* | Round | Her pride and prejudice make her reversal believable |
| Raskolnikov | *Crime and Punishment* | Round | Forster names Dostoevsky's characters as round, and Raskolnikov's guilt and conviction pull against each other |

## Final words

Pick the depth each character needs, and spend your effort where the story needs it. Your protagonist deserves contradiction and surprise. The people around them can be sharp, consistent and flat, so long as each one does a job.
