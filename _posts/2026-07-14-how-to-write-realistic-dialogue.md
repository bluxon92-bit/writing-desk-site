The first piece of feedback many writers get about their dialogue is some version of "it's a bit... expository." Characters who speak in paragraphs. Who explain things to each other that they both already know. Who say exactly what they mean and mean exactly what they say. Nobody arguing properly, nobody dodging a question, nobody saying the wrong thing at the wrong moment and having to live with it.

That's writing people who don't sound like people. Here's what actually fixes it.

## Dialogue Is Not Conversation

Real conversation is inefficient. People repeat themselves. They trail off. They say "um" and "you know" and circle back to things they forgot to mention. Transcribed verbatim, real conversation is almost unreadable.

Good fictional dialogue is conversation that's been distilled. It sounds natural, but it's doing several things simultaneously: revealing character, advancing plot, building tension, layering subtext. Every line earns its place.

If a line of dialogue doesn't do at least one of those things, cut it. Not trim it. Cut it.

## Understanding the Role of Dialogue

### Character Development

Dialogue provides an excellent opportunity to reveal [character traits](/blog/character-traits/) and motivations. Through speech patterns, word choices, and tone, characters' personalities shine through. Consider how each character's background, values, and experiences influence their dialogue, creating unique voices readers can connect with.

### Plot Advancement

Dialogue is an effective tool for advancing the plot. It lets characters discuss and make decisions, convey important information, and introduce conflicts and obstacles. Used strategically, it propels the story forward and maintains a compelling pace.

### Subtext and Conflict

Dialogue can convey more than the literal words spoken. It can be infused with subtext, underlying meanings, and hidden tensions. Characters may say one thing while feeling or implying something entirely different. Mastering subtext is what deepens the complexity of dialogue and creates layers of intrigue.

> **Writing Desk tip:** The AI Editor's line edit mode specifically looks at dialogue clarity and subtext, flagging exchanges that feel on-the-nose or where the subtext isn't landing. Worth running after any emotionally charged scene.

## How to Make Your Dialogue More Realistic

### 1. Study Real-Life Conversations

To write dialogue that feels authentic, observe how people actually speak: the rhythm, the pauses, the interruptions, the casual language of everyday conversation. Immersing yourself in real speech is what lets you capture the nuances that make dialogue feel genuine on the page.

### 2. Develop Distinct Voices for Each Character

Each character should have a voice that reflects their personality, background, and experience. Age, education, regional dialect, and social status all shape how someone talks. Use distinct speech patterns, vocabulary, and sentence structure to differentiate characters, so readers can identify who's speaking without a tag.

### 3. Use Appropriate Language and Vocabulary

Language should be appropriate to a character's background and context; a well-educated professor speaks differently than a street-smart teenager. Reflect individual knowledge, interests, and social circles through dialogue, without overdoing dialects or accents to the point of distraction.

### 4. Incorporate Non-Verbal Cues

Dialogue isn't solely about the words spoken. It's also about what remains unsaid. Body language, facial expressions, gestures, and tone of voice can convey meaning and emotion that the words alone don't carry.

### 5. Use Action Beats

Action beats are small movements that occur alongside or between lines of dialogue. They break up dialogue, provide visual interest, and reveal character without a dialogue tag doing the work.

*John smiled and leaned back in his chair. "I've been waiting for this moment."*

*Mary's eyes widened, and she gripped the edge of the table. "What do you mean?"*

The action beats give the reader a visual cue and suggest subtext, showing the characters' emotional states rather than telling them.

## Final Words

Realistic dialogue isn't a transcript of how people actually talk; it's a distillation of it, shaped so every line reveals character, advances plot, or builds tension, usually all three at once. Study real speech, give every character a distinct voice, and let what's unsaid carry as much weight as what is.
