POV broke my second novel.

I'd written sixty thousand words in close third person before I admitted to myself that the story was fighting the choice. The protagonist's inner life, her unreliability, the gap between what she believed and what was actually happening, required first person. The intimacy, the confined perspective, the voice. In third person, she kept slipping into the background of her own story.

I rewrote it. Every scene. It took three months and was completely worth it.

POV isn't a stylistic preference. It's a structural decision that shapes every sentence in your novel. Choose wrong and you'll feel the friction for the entire draft. This is what I wish someone had explained to me before I made that mistake.

---

## What POV Actually Does

Point of view determines who is telling the story, and from what position. That choice controls what information your reader has access to, how closely they're aligned with your protagonist, what kind of narrative voice is possible, and what you as the writer can and cannot show.

Most discussions of POV focus on the pronouns, "I" versus "she" versus "you." The pronouns matter, but they're downstream of a more fundamental question: how much does the narrator know, and how much distance sits between reader and character?

---

## First Person

*"I walked into the bar and knew immediately it was a mistake."*

The story is told by a character inside it, usually the protagonist. The reader experiences everything through their perception, their voice, their limitations.

**What it does well:** Intimacy and voice. First person is the closest you can get to putting a reader inside a character's head. It's the natural choice for stories where the protagonist's perspective is the point, [unreliable narrator](/blog/unreliable-narrator/)s, distinctive voices, deeply internal journeys, coming-of-age stories where the narrator's particular way of seeing the world is what the book is about.

**What it costs you:** Everything outside your narrator's knowledge. You cannot show scenes they don't witness. You cannot reveal what other characters are thinking unless your narrator interprets it (and they might be wrong). You're committed to their perception even when their perception is flawed, which is a feature, not a bug, but it takes discipline.

**Common mistake:** Forgetting that first person narrators are unreliable by nature. They interpret, misread, and miss things. Writers who treat first person as a transparent window onto events miss what makes it powerful.

**Works well for:** Literary fiction, crime and thriller with a strongly voiced detective, YA, horror, any story where the narrator's interiority is the subject.

---

## Third Person Limited

*"She walked into the bar and knew immediately it was a mistake."*

The story is narrated from outside the character, "she," "he," "they", but the camera sits close. The reader experiences one character's perceptions, thoughts, and emotions at a time, without the narrator knowing more than that character does.

This is the dominant POV in commercial fiction for good reason. It gives you the intimacy of first person with slightly more narrative flexibility. You can describe your protagonist from the outside in ways first person makes awkward. You can control what readers know by controlling what the viewpoint character notices.

**What it does well:** Balance. You get close enough for emotional engagement, far enough for some narrative control. It also makes switching POV between chapters much cleaner than first person, each chapter can sit inside a different character's limited perspective.

**What it costs you:** The distinctive narrator voice that first person makes possible. Third limited can have voice, but it lives in the prose rather than in a character narrating retrospectively.

**Common mistake:** Head-hopping, jumping between characters' thoughts within a single scene without signalling the shift. Readers can track one mind at a time. Stay in one head per scene.

**Works well for:** Most commercial fiction, fantasy, thriller, romance, literary fiction. If you're not sure which POV to use, start here.

---

## Third Person Omniscient

*"She walked into the bar. She didn't know what the bartender thought when he saw her, but he recognised her immediately."*

The narrator knows everything, every character's thoughts, every off-screen event, the full picture. This was the dominant mode of nineteenth-century novels. Austen, Dickens, Tolstoy all used it.

It's less common now, and for practical reasons. Readers have come to expect deep immersion in a single perspective. An omniscient narrator can feel detached, you know more, but you feel less. The camera is always slightly pulled back.

**What it does well:** Epic scope. Multiple protagonists with equal weight. Irony, the narrator knowing things the characters don't creates dramatic tension that limited POV can't access in the same way.

**What it costs you:** Intimacy. Done poorly, omniscient feels like a god narrating events to you rather than a character living through them.

**Common mistake:** Sliding into omniscient accidentally when writing third limited. If your limited third suddenly knows what a character across the room is thinking, you've drifted.

**Works well for:** Epic fantasy, saga-style historical fiction, stories with large ensemble casts where no single character is the clear emotional centre.

---

## Second Person

*"You walk into the bar and know immediately it's a mistake."*

The narrator addresses the reader as "you." It's rare in novel-length fiction for good reason, it's difficult to sustain and some readers find it gimmicky. But when it works, it creates an uncanny intimacy that no other POV can match.

I've used it for short fiction. I wouldn't attempt a full novel in second person without a very specific reason for why the story couldn't be told any other way.

**Works well for:** Experimental literary fiction, psychological horror where you want the reader to feel implicated, short fiction.

---

## How to Choose

The frameworks above are useful, but the real test is simpler: write the same scene in first and third limited and see which one feels right. The one that feels right usually is.

A few questions that help me decide:

**Is your protagonist's voice the point?** If the story lives or dies on how this specific person narrates their experience, use first person.

**Do you need to cut between multiple characters' interiors?** Third limited with multiple viewpoint characters (one per chapter or scene) is cleaner than first person for ensemble casts.

**Is your story about dramatic irony, the reader knowing things the characters don't?** Omniscient handles this best.

**Does your genre have conventions?** YA skews first person. Epic fantasy skews third limited or omniscient. Literary fiction uses all three. Romance skews third limited. Check the shelf you want to sit on.

---

## Consistency Is the Rule

Whatever you choose, stay consistent within a scene. Head-hopping, moving between characters' thoughts without signalling the shift, is the most common POV error in unpublished fiction, and it's disorienting every time.

If you're switching viewpoint characters between chapters, make it clear at the chapter heading or in the first sentence. Readers can track multiple perspectives. They can't track unmarked perspective shifts mid-scene.
