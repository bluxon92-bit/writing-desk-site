Every novel has a main plot. The big engine, the central conflict your protagonist is driving toward. But most novels that feel rich and real have something else running underneath: subplots.

Here's what they are, why they matter, and how to write them so they actually do something useful.

## What Is a Subplot?

A subplot is a secondary storyline that runs alongside your main plot. It might involve your protagonist or a supporting character. It has its own beginning, middle, and end, it's not just a scene or a side detail. And at its best, it intersects with the main plot in meaningful ways.

Think of subplots as the supporting cast of your structure. The main plot answers the central question of your novel. The subplots complicate it, deepen it, and reflect it.

## What Makes a Subplot Worth Having?

Not every subplot justifies its existence. The ones that do share a few qualities:

They're connected to the theme. A subplot about a secondary character's failing marriage, in a novel about the illusion of control, isn't a distraction, it's a thematic echo. The best subplots reinforce what your main story is really about.

They create complications for the main plot. A good subplot doesn't run in a separate lane. It merges, crosses, and sometimes crashes into the main road. The events of your subplot should make your protagonist's central problem harder or more complicated.

They have their own arc. A subplot needs a beginning (introducing the conflict), a middle (escalation and complication), and an end (resolution). If it just trails off, it'll feel unfinished. If it never builds, it'll feel like filler.

## Common Types of Subplots

The romantic subplot, a relationship that develops alongside the main story. Works best when the relationship dynamics directly mirror or complicate the central conflict.

The character [backstory](/blog/backstory/) subplot, exploring a secondary character's past and how it's driving their present behaviour. This is a good way to deepen characters who'd otherwise feel functional.

The internal conflict subplot, your protagonist's private struggle that exists beneath the external plot. Often the most powerful kind.

## Keeping Track of Everything

The biggest challenge with subplots isn't writing them, it's managing them. Once you have two or three running simultaneously, it's easy to lose threads, let one go quiet for fifty pages, or forget to resolve it.

> **Writing Desk tip:** A proper story outline is what saves you here. In the Story Plotter you can layer subplot structure directly into your scene-by-scene outline, so you can see at a glance where each subplot surfaces and whether it's getting the attention it needs.

A subplot that's well-integrated and properly resolved is one of the things that makes a novel feel full. Readers can't always articulate it, but they feel the difference between a story that's one-dimensional and one that has depth running underneath.
