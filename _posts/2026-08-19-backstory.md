Backstory is everything that happened to a character before the story you're currently telling begins: their history, their relationships, the events that shaped who they are on page one. Every character has one, whether or not the reader ever sees most of it.

The problem backstory creates isn't its existence. It's the temptation to deliver it all at once, usually early, usually as a block of explanation that stops the forward motion of the story to bring the reader up to speed.

## Why Writers Over-Deliver It

Backstory feels important to the writer because they've usually spent a lot of time developing it, often more than what actually makes it onto the page. That imbalance creates pressure to justify the work by including it, which is exactly the instinct worth resisting. The reader doesn't need to know everything you know about a character. They need to know what's relevant, when it's relevant.

## How to Reveal It Without Stopping the Story

**Drip it in through action and dialogue**, rather than narrative summary. A character flinching at a raised voice tells the reader something happened without a paragraph explaining what.

**Reveal it when it's earned, not when it's convenient.** Backstory lands hardest when the plot has already made the reader want to know it. A character's reason for distrusting authority means more once the reader has watched them refuse to trust someone in the present-day story.

**Use it to answer a question the reader is already asking.** If a character's behaviour in the current scene doesn't make sense without context, that's the moment backstory earns its place. Delivered before the reader has a reason to want it, the same information reads as a stall.

**Cut anything that doesn't change how the reader understands the present.** A rule of thumb: if a piece of backstory could be removed entirely without the reader misunderstanding a single choice the character makes going forward, it probably doesn't need to be on the page at all.

## Backstory vs. a Prologue or Flashback

Backstory is the material itself, the history that exists whether or not it's shown. A [prologue](/blog/what-is-a-prologue/) and a [flashback](/blog/flashback/) are two of the tools available for delivering it directly as a scene, rather than through allusion in the present-day story. Most backstory is better delivered through allusion; full dramatised scenes from the past are worth reserving for moments significant enough to justify stopping the current story's momentum.

## Final Words

Good backstory is almost always invisible as backstory. It arrives in fragments, exactly when the reader needs them, and never reads like the author pausing the story to explain something. If a reader could summarise a character's relevant history accurately without ever having been told it directly, that's usually a sign the backstory was delivered well.
