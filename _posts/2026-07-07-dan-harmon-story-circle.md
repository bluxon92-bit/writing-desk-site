If you've ever finished a first draft and thought, "something's off, but I can't figure out what," you're not alone. Structure is usually the culprit. And Dan Harmon's Story Circle might be the clearest, most practical fix out there.

Harmon is the creator of *Community* and *Rick and Morty*. Not a novelist. But his story framework, a stripped-back riff on Joseph Campbell's [Hero's Journey](/blog/heros-journey/), has quietly become one of the most useful tools in any fiction writer's kit, novel or otherwise. It's simple enough to hold in your head, deep enough to add real depth to your work, and flexible enough to work across genres.

Here's how it works, and how to use it.

## What Is Dan Harmon's Story Circle?

Dan Harmon's Story Circle is a narrative structure arranged, as the name suggests, in a circle. It maps the journey of a character from their ordinary world, through disruption and struggle, to transformation and return.

The eight steps are:

1. **You:** a character in a zone of comfort
2. **Need:** but they want something
3. **Go:** they enter an unfamiliar situation
4. **Search:** adapt to it
5. **Find:** get what they wanted
6. **Take:** pay a heavy price for it
7. **Return:** then return to their familiar situation
8. **Change:** having changed

That's it. Eight points on a circle. But don't let the simplicity fool you, the insight baked into this framework is genuinely profound.

## Why a Circle?

The circular shape isn't arbitrary. It reflects the way transformation often works in stories: the character ends where they began, but they're not the same person.

The circle is also divided into two halves. The top half is the "above the line" world, the familiar, the comfortable, the known. The bottom half is the "below the line" world, the unfamiliar, the difficult, the transformative. Your character descends into chaos and, if they're lucky, makes it back out.

This maps loosely onto the classic structure of myth. But Harmon's genius was stripping away the academic scaffolding and making it immediately usable.

## The Eight Steps in Detail

### 1. You, establish who your character is

Before anything happens, the reader needs to meet your protagonist in their natural state. Not their best state. Not their worst. Just their normal.

This is about establishing a baseline. Who is this person? What do they believe about the world? What are their habits, their relationships, their comfort zone?

The trap here is over-explaining. You don't need to dump backstory. A few specific, telling details will do more than three pages of biography. Show them in action, at home, at work, in a relationship, and let the reader draw their own conclusions.

One thing worth noting: "zone of comfort" doesn't mean happy, and it doesn't mean boring, easy, or without conflict or tension. A character can be miserable and still be in their zone of comfort. Their misery is familiar to them. They know how to be miserable. That can be just as much of an anchor as happiness.

### 2. Need, what do they want?

Your character wants something. This is the engine of your story.

But there's a distinction that's easy to miss: the want and the need are rarely the same thing. The want is what your character thinks will fix things. The need is what will actually fix them, often something they can't see yet.

This gap between want and need is where character depth lives. A character who wants fame but needs to be seen. A character who wants revenge but needs to forgive. The Story Circle works because it's fundamentally about that gap being slowly, painfully closed.

Make sure you know both before you start writing. The want drives your plot. The need drives your theme.

### 3. Go, enter the unfamiliar

Your character crosses a threshold. They leave the comfortable world and step into something new. A new city, a new relationship, a new identity, a new set of rules.

This is the inciting incident in traditional story terms. Something changes. The old equilibrium is broken.

The "go" moment doesn't have to be physical. It can be emotional or psychological. A character who discovers their partner has been lying to them has "gone" somewhere new, even if they're standing in the same kitchen.

The key here is commitment. Your character has to actually step through the door, not hover on the threshold for three chapters.

### 4. Search, adapt and struggle

This is where most of your story lives. Your character is in unfamiliar territory and trying to make sense of it. They're adapting, failing, trying again.

The "search" phase is about attempting to get what they want using the tools and beliefs they arrived with, the coping mechanisms from their old world. But those tools won't fully fit this new world. Not yet. Because getting what they need will require the character to adapt those tools, to become someone different.

This phase should be full of setbacks, complications, and rising stakes. Every attempt to solve the problem should create a new, slightly worse problem. That's the job.

This is also where your subplots tend to live. Relationships that complicate the main quest. Secondary characters who reflect different aspects of your protagonist's dilemma, or whose motivations conflict with the protagonist's.

### 5. Find, get what you wanted

The character finally gets the thing they've been chasing. The goal is achieved.

But here's where the Story Circle starts to show its strength. Getting the want doesn't automatically satisfy the need. In fact, the moment of getting is often the moment when the gap between want and need becomes most painfully visible.

In some stories, this is a moment of apparent triumph that quickly turns hollow. In others, it's the thing they've been building toward, but the cost of it becomes clear in step six.

### 6. Take, pay the price

This is the most important step, and the one most often underwritten.

Your character has what they wanted. Now they have to pay for it. Something is sacrificed: a relationship, a belief, an old version of themselves. Whatever you make it, the price has to be real, and it has to hurt.

This is the moment of maximum cost. Often it corresponds to the dark night of the soul in three-act terms. It's the lowest point, the moment where the character has to decide who they really are and what they're really willing to sacrifice.

Don't shortchange this moment. If the price isn't high enough, the transformation won't feel earned. Readers are more sophisticated than we give them credit for. They can feel when a character's change hasn't cost anything.

### 7. Return, go back

The character returns to the familiar world. But they're bringing something back with them: knowledge, a changed perspective, an object, a scar.

The "return" can be literal or metaphorical. What matters is that the character re-enters the space of their ordinary world transformed. They see it differently now. The same people, the same places, the same conflicts, but through new eyes.

This is where the thematic statement of your novel often crystallises. What does the character now know that they didn't before?

### 8. Change, they are not who they were

The circle closes. The character is back where they started, but irrevocably different.

Ideally, this change should feel both surprising and inevitable. Surprising, because we didn't fully see it coming. Inevitable, because, looking back, we can see exactly how it happened, step by step.

The change should also be specific. Not just "they're wiser now." What, concretely, do they believe differently? What would they do differently if they faced step two again?

## Why the Story Circle Works for Novelists

The most common objection I hear is: "but that's a screenwriting tool." Sure, Harmon developed it for TV. But the underlying logic, transformation through encounter with the unfamiliar, at a cost, applies to any story.

What makes it especially useful for novelists is its flexibility. Unlike three-act structure or the Hero's Journey in its longer forms, the Story Circle is compact enough that you can apply it at multiple levels: to your whole novel, mapping the protagonist's arc across the full book; to individual acts or parts, mapping the arc within a section; to subplots, mapping secondary characters' journeys in miniature; or to individual scenes, as a micro-structure for a chapter.

That last one is particularly useful. Each chapter can be thought of as its own tiny Story Circle: a character in a state, with a want, who enters the chapter, searches, finds, pays a price, and exits changed. It won't always work perfectly, of course, some chapters are more about momentum than transformation, but using it as a test can help you spot the ones that fall flat, the ones without enough conflict, tension, or stakes.

## Running Multiple Story Circles

Longer novels with ensemble casts often run several Story Circles simultaneously, one per major character. The art is in making sure they intersect at the right moments, and that the costs paid by one character resonate with the journeys of the others.

## The Story Circle vs. Three-Act Structure

You've probably heard of [three-act structure](/blog/three-act-structure/): setup, confrontation, resolution. And you've probably used it, consciously or not. So how does the Story Circle relate?

Think of the Story Circle as a zoom-in on what's happening beneath the surface of three-act structure. The three acts give you the shape of the plot; the Story Circle gives you the shape of the character.

If you're finding that your plot feels solid but your characters feel flat, that's often because you've been writing in three-act terms and haven't mapped the psychological journey underneath. The Story Circle forces you to ask: at every stage, what is this person experiencing, not just doing?

## Common Mistakes

**Skipping the "Take" step.** Characters who get what they want without meaningful sacrifice feel unearned. If your protagonist comes through relatively unscathed, go back and ask what they should have lost.

**Making the "Need" too abstract.** "She needs to learn to love herself" is too vague to build a story around. Anchor needs in concrete behaviour and specific relationships.

**Confusing the inner and outer story.** The outer story is the plot, the sequence of events. The inner story is the transformation. They should work in parallel, each one making the other more meaningful. If the external plot is nailed but the character's change feels arbitrary, the inner journey probably needs more work.

**Forgetting the return.** Some writers are so focused on the transformation that they rush the ending. The return matters. The character needs to re-enter their ordinary world and demonstrate how they've changed.

## A Practical Exercise: Mapping Your Novel

Sit down with your manuscript, if draft one is done, or your outline, and try to identify each of the eight steps.

If you can articulate all eight clearly, the bones of your story are probably solid.

If you find yourself stuck on step six, the price, or step eight, the specific change, that's useful information. That's where the work is.

If you can't find steps three or seven, the crossing of thresholds, your story might be lacking genuine disruption or a real return to stakes.

You don't need to be rigid about it. These steps aren't frames to force your story into. Use them as diagnostic lenses, a way to find where your story is already strong and where it needs to be pushed harder.

## Story Circle Examples in Fiction

**The Road by Cormac McCarthy.** The father, You, is in a devastated post-apocalyptic world, Need: keep his son alive and maintain his own humanity. They travel south, Go, through a series of encounters that test every moral limit, Search. They find the coast, the goal they've aimed at, Find. The father dies, Take. The son is taken in by another family, Return and Change: the father's sacrifice transforms into the son's survival and the continuation of something like hope.

**Jane Eyre by Charlotte Brontë.** Jane, You, is a plain, independent woman in an unfair world, Need: love without losing herself. She goes to Thornfield, Go, falls for Rochester, navigates secrets and class, Search, agrees to marry him, Find, discovers his wife in the attic and everything collapses, Take, leaves and eventually returns to Rochester, Return, as his equal, on her own terms, Change.

**The Great Gatsby by F. Scott Fitzgerald.** Interestingly, Gatsby fails the Story Circle. He goes, to wealth, to the green light, to Daisy, searches, appears to find, and then pays the highest price. But he never truly returns or changes. That's also the point of the story. His inability to complete the circle, to actually transform, is the tragedy. Some stories use the circle to show what happens when someone can't complete it.

## Final Thoughts

The Story Circle is a question you keep asking your manuscript: does this character change, and have they paid a real price for it?

If the answer is yes, if you can trace each of the eight steps and find them all doing meaningful work, you probably have a story that will resonate.

If the answer is vague, or if some steps are missing entirely, now you know where to dig.

That's the value of a structure like this.
