The choice between self-publishing and traditional publishing comes down to five real trade-offs: timeline, money, control, distribution, and how much marketing lands on you. Everything else people argue about online is detail sitting on top of those five things.

## What Traditional Publishing Offers

A publisher buys the rights to your book, typically through an advance against future royalties, and handles editing, cover design, printing, distribution, and at least some marketing. In exchange, you give up creative control over most of those decisions and a large share of the revenue.

**The real numbers.** Royalties typically run 7 to 25% of the cover price, and most of that gets paid out slowly against the advance you already received, meaning you often don't see further royalty payments until the book has earned back what you were advanced. Advances vary enormously by genre, track record, and publisher size, from a few thousand pounds for a debut at a small press to substantial sums for a big-five deal with an established author.

**The real timeline.** Getting an agent typically takes months of querying. Getting a publishing deal after that takes longer. Once a deal is signed, the book itself is often 12 to 24 months from contract to shelf, publishers plan catalogues far in advance, and your release date fits their schedule, not yours.

**What you're paying for with that lost control and time.** A professional editorial team, a design team, a sales force with existing relationships at bookshops and distributors, and access to review coverage and marketing channels that are difficult for an individual author to reach alone.

## What Self-Publishing Offers

You keep the rights, make every creative decision, keep the vast majority of the revenue, and set your own timeline. You also take on every cost and every piece of the process that a publisher would otherwise have handled.

**The real numbers.** KDP royalties run 35% or 70% depending on price point (70% requires pricing between $2.99 and $9.99 and enrolling in the relevant territories), which is dramatically higher per copy than a traditional royalty rate. There's no advance, so every pound spent on editing, covers, and formatting comes from you upfront, with no guarantee of earning it back.

**The real timeline.** As fast as the work itself takes. A finished, edited, formatted manuscript can be live on KDP within days of upload. The constraint is how long the actual editing and production work takes, not a publishing schedule, and that work is still substantial. See [what self-publishing costs](/blog/cost-to-self-publish/) for the full breakdown of that work.

**What you're taking on in exchange for that speed and control.** Every editorial decision, every design decision, and every piece of marketing. There's no sales team with existing bookshop relationships. Discoverability is entirely down to your own effort, categories, keywords, ARC readers, and whatever marketing you're willing to do yourself.

## The Trade-offs, Side by Side

**Timeline.** Self-publishing: weeks to months. Traditional: one to two years or more from deal to release, on top of however long it took to land the deal.

**Money per copy.** Self-publishing: 35 to 70% royalty, no advance, all production costs upfront and unrecovered until sales happen. Traditional: 7 to 25% royalty, an advance paid upfront, production costs covered by the publisher.

**Creative control.** Self-publishing: total, cover, title, editing choices, pricing, release date, all yours. Traditional: shared at best, a publisher's editorial and design teams have real input, and final say often isn't yours.

**Distribution and discoverability.** Self-publishing: primarily online retail, built through your own categories, keywords, and marketing. Traditional: physical bookshop placement and existing distributor relationships that are extremely difficult to access independently.

**Validation and gatekeeping.** Self-publishing: none required, and none granted, either. Traditional: an agent and a publisher both said yes, which carries real weight with certain readers, reviewers, and award juries, whether or not that weight is fair.

**Risk.** Self-publishing: financial risk sits with you, all of it. Traditional: financial risk sits mostly with the publisher, they've already paid you.

## Hybrid Approaches Exist

Plenty of working authors do both, self-publishing one series while traditionally publishing another, or self-publishing backlist titles that reverted to them after a traditional deal ended. The two paths aren't mutually exclusive across a career.

## How to Decide

**Do you need the money now, or can you wait?** An advance is money before the book sells a single copy. Self-publishing pays nothing until readers buy it, and production costs come out of your own pocket first.

**Do you want the ceiling or the floor raised?** Traditional publishing's ceiling, wide bookshop distribution, major review coverage, is higher than self-publishing reaches on its own. Self-publishing's floor, a book that exists and earns something, is more reliably available than landing a traditional deal at all.

**How much do you want to control?** If a specific cover, a specific release date, or creative control over editorial choices matters more to you than a traditional deal's other benefits, that's a real answer, not a lesser one.

**Do you have, or want to build, the skills and budget self-publishing requires?** Editing, cover design, formatting, and marketing all need to happen either way. Traditionally, someone else does them. Self-published, you're hiring for them, doing them yourself, or some mix of both.

> **Writing Desk tip:** Whichever path you take, the manuscript itself needs to be finished and properly self-edited before it goes anywhere, to an agent, a publisher, or straight to KDP. [How to self-edit your novel](/blog/self-edit-novel/) covers the three passes worth running first.

## Final Words

Neither path is the correct one in the abstract. Traditional publishing trades money and control for reach and validation you can't easily build alone. Self-publishing trades that reach and validation for speed, control, and a much larger share of the money on every copy sold. The right choice depends on what you want from this particular book, not on which path sounds more legitimate.
