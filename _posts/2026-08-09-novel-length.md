There's no single correct length for a novel, but there are strong genre conventions, and straying too far outside them creates real practical problems, particularly if you're planning to query agents or list a self-published book in a category readers have expectations for.

## Typical Word Counts by Genre

**Literary fiction:** 80,000 to 110,000 words. Literary fiction has the most flexibility of any category, and exceptions well outside this range are common, but this is the range most debut literary novels fall into.

**Commercial and genre fiction (thriller, mystery, romance):** 70,000 to 100,000 words. Romance in particular tends to sit at the tighter end of this range, especially in category and series romance.

**Science fiction and fantasy:** 90,000 to 120,000 words. Epic fantasy routinely runs longer, sometimes well past 150,000 words, because the sub-genre's readers expect and want that scale. First novels in a fantasy series are usually held closer to the standard range even when later books in the same series grow.

**Young adult:** 50,000 to 80,000 words, with contemporary YA at the lower end and YA fantasy at the higher end.

**Middle grade:** 30,000 to 55,000 words, varying significantly by age band within the category.

**Short novels / novellas:** Under 50,000 words. Below roughly 40,000 words, a manuscript is generally considered a novella rather than a novel, which matters for how it's categorised and marketed.

## Why the Ranges Matter

**Querying agents.** A debut manuscript significantly outside genre norms, especially significantly over, is one of the most common reasons agents pass without reading further. A 180,000-word debut thriller reads as a manuscript that hasn't been edited down to its essential shape, regardless of how good the prose is.

**Reader expectations.** Genre readers develop a felt sense of how long a book in their preferred category should take to read, and a book that's dramatically shorter or longer than expected can feel like it's not delivering what the cover promised, even if nothing else about it is wrong.

**Self-publishing categories.** Retailers and categorisation systems often have length thresholds that determine which category a book is even eligible to appear in, which has direct discoverability consequences.

**Print costs.** For anyone planning a physical print run, page count drives per-unit cost directly, and very long manuscripts become measurably more expensive to produce.

## What If Your Draft Is Too Long or Too Short?

**Too long usually means structural problems, not sentence-level ones.** Before cutting at the word or sentence level, check whether entire [subplot](/blog/what-is-a-subplot/)s or point-of-view threads are earning their space. A subplot that doesn't meaningfully intersect with the main plot is often the fastest way to cut 10,000 words at once. See [what a subplot is and how to write one](/blog/what-is-a-subplot/) for what a subplot needs to be doing to justify its page count.

**Too short usually means underdeveloped conflict, not missing scenes.** A manuscript that comes in thin is rarely fixed by adding more events. It's more often fixed by giving existing scenes more room: more [pacing](/blog/pacing/) at moments that matter, more interiority, a subplot that deepens rather than pads.

## Chapter Length and Count

There's no fixed rule for chapter length, but most published novels run somewhere between 2,000 and 5,000 words per chapter, with genre [pacing](/blog/pacing/) (thriller chapters tend shorter, literary fiction chapters tend to vary more) as the biggest influence. A novel doesn't need consistent chapter lengths throughout. Varying chapter length deliberately, a short, punchy chapter after several longer ones, is itself a pacing tool.

## Final Words

Word count is a means to an end, not the goal itself. The right length for your novel is the length the story actually needs to work, and genre conventions exist because they roughly describe what most stories in that category need. Treat the ranges above as a sanity check once a draft is finished, not a target to write toward from page one.
