Every scene in a novel has a point of view. You can think of it, and third person gives you two main places to put it. You can sit on a character's shoulder and tell the story through what they notice, or you can rise above the whole story and see everything at once. Third person limited and third person omniscient make the same promise of *he*, *she* and *they*, and they give the reader very different experiences.

This guide explains how each one works, shows both in published novels, covers the head-hopping trap and gives you a checklist for choosing between them.

## What is third person point of view?

In third person, a narrator outside the story tells it using *he*, *she*, *it* and *they*. Unlike [first person](/blog/first-person-pov/), the narrator stands outside the cast as a voice, which gives you flexibility over how much of the story's world and how many minds the reader can see.

Three variations cover most of what you will read:

- **Third person limited.** The narrator stays with one character at a time and reports only what that character perceives, thinks and feels.
- **Third person omniscient.** The narrator knows everything: every character's thoughts, events no one witnesses and what the future holds.
- **Third person objective.** The narrator reports only what could be seen and heard, like a camera, and never enters a character's mind. Hemingway's short story "Hills Like White Elephants" is a well-known example.

For the wider picture, see our guide to [point of view](/blog/point-of-view/).

| | Third person limited | Third person omniscient |
|---|---|---|
| Whose mind can the narrator enter? | One character at a time | Any character, at any time |
| What can the narrator report? | What that character knows and perceives | Anything, including events no character sees |
| Closeness to the character | Close | Varies, often wider |
| Scope of the story | Narrower, built scene by scene | Broad, across many people and places |
| Narrator's own voice | Usually quiet | Can have a distinct personality |
| Suspense | The reader learns as the character does | The narrator can reveal what characters do not know |
| Difficulty | Easier to keep consistent | Needs a controlled narrator and clear transitions |

## What is third person limited?

Third person limited, also called close third, stays inside one character's perspective for a scene or a chapter. The narrator sees through that character's eyes and cannot report what they do not know. Everything else, including other characters' feelings, reaches the reader through what the point-of-view character observes and infers.

Harry Potter's story is told this way for most of the series, so the reader learns about Hogwarts as Harry does. George Orwell's *Nineteen Eighty-Four* stays largely with Winston Smith, and the reader's growing unease mirrors his.

Limited narration works with a single point of view character, and it works with several. In *A Game of Thrones*, each chapter is titled with the name of the character whose perspective it uses, so the reader always knows whose head they are in. That gives George R.R. Martin a large cast and a close view of each member.

**Deep third** is the tightest form. The narrator's voice almost merges with the character's, so thoughts and perceptions arrive without tags like *she thought* or *he felt*. It shares the intimacy of first person and keeps the flexibility of third. Because deep third works at close range, [filter words](/blog/filter-words/) can undercut it.

## What is third person omniscient?

In third person omniscient, the narrator stands outside the story with a view over all of it. The narrator can move between characters' minds, describe events that happen out of sight and comment on the story, the characters or the world. It is sometimes called the god's-eye view.

That freedom opens up several effects:

- **Breadth.** You can follow many characters across places and years, as Tolstoy does in [*War and Peace*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2600).
- **Dramatic irony.** The narrator can tell the reader what characters do not know, so the reader watches a mistake approach. That gap is a classic way to [create tension](/blog/how-to-create-tension/).
- **Narrator as character.** The narrator can have a voice, a worldview and a sense of humour. In Terry Pratchett's Discworld novels, the narrator pulls back to wide views and comic asides that no single character could offer.
- **Commentary and wisdom.** George Eliot's narrator in [*Middlemarch*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/145) steps in with reflections and, in one famous passage, asks why the story should always stay with Dorothea before turning to Casaubon's inner life.

## Examples of third person omniscient in published fiction

- ***Middlemarch* by George Eliot.** A narrator with a distinct moral voice moves across a whole community and comments on what it sees.
- ***War and Peace* by Leo Tolstoy.** The narrator follows dozens of characters through private life and war and steps back to reflect on history.
- ***The Hobbit* by J.R.R. Tolkien.** The narrator addresses the reader directly and shares information Bilbo does not have.
- **Discworld by Terry Pratchett.** The narrator's comic, wide-angle voice builds the world and its jokes together.
- ***Pride and Prejudice* by Jane Austen.** The famous opening sentence speaks with an omniscient narrator's authority, and the story then spends most of its time close to Elizabeth Bennet. Austen shows that a narrator can move between the two.

## How does psychic distance affect the choice?

The novelist John Gardner, in *The Art of Fiction*, described psychic distance: how close the narration sits to a character's mind, from a wide establishing shot to the inside of a thought. Both limited and omniscient narrators can move along that scale.

**Far:** The town lay quiet under a grey sky, and everyone in it was asleep or pretending to be.
**Middle:** Mara crossed the square with her head down.
**Close:** Mara's pulse hammered. Don't run. Don't look back.

![Psychic distance scale from far to close, with three example lines and ranges for omniscient and limited narration](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnkddcxrt/image/upload/v1794780649/psychic-distance-dial_ap32vo.svg)

Omniscient narration moves freely along the whole scale, often within a page. Limited narration usually sits in the middle or close, and pulls back for scene-setting. Distance is one of the most useful dials in third person. Pulling back covers time and space quickly, and moving close slows the moment down, so it doubles as a [pacing](/blog/pacing/) tool. Knowing where each scene sits keeps the narration steady.

## What is head-hopping, and how do you avoid it?

Head-hopping means shifting between characters' thoughts within a scene with no signal to the reader. The result is a reader who cannot tell whose thoughts they are in.

**Head-hopped:** Mara hated the way Tom stared at her. Tom wished she would smile and regretted the joke.

Two minds sit in two sentences, and the narrator offers no frame for the shift. Here are two fixes.

**Stay in limited:** Mara hated the way Tom stared at her. He kept glancing at her mouth as if waiting for her to smile, and the joke he had just made was clearly haunting him.

Mara infers Tom's feelings from what she sees, so the scene holds a single point of view.

**Go omniscient on purpose:** Mara hated the way Tom stared at her. Tom, for his part, wished she would smile, a wish that would have astonished her.

Here the narrator is visibly in charge, steps above both characters and uses the gap between them for irony. The difference between head-hopping and controlled omniscience is control: omniscient narration shifts with a clear narrator voice that guides the reader.

To keep limited narration clean:

- **One point of view per scene.** Hold it until the scene ends. [Scene and sequel](/blog/scene-and-sequel/) covers how to shape each scene.
- **Signal a change with a break.** Use a scene break or a chapter break when you move to a new character. Our guide to [words per chapter](/blog/words-per-chapter/) covers using chapters to carry a switch.
- **Show other characters through what the viewpoint character sees.** Dialogue, gestures and expressions can carry a secondary character's feelings.

## Can you mix third person limited and omniscient?

Yes, and published novels do. A common pattern opens wide and then closes in. The first chapter of *Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone* follows Mr Dursley, pulls back to the street and its strange events, and moves among the people who gather there, before the story settles into Harry's close perspective. *Pride and Prejudice* does something similar on a smaller scale.

If you mix, do it with intent. Establish the pattern early, so the reader learns the narrator's habits, and keep it consistent through the book.

## When should you choose third person limited?

Choose third person limited when:

- **Your story rests on a few characters' inner lives.** Close access to thought and feeling is the main strength.
- **You want the reader to learn as the character learns.** Mystery, suspense and discovery all benefit.
- **You want intimacy without a narrator's voice.** Deep third gives close contact while the narrator stays out of the way.
- **You plan a rotating cast.** One character per scene or chapter keeps each viewpoint clear, as in *A Game of Thrones*. Each point-of-view character needs a [character arc](/blog/character-arc/) of their own.

## When should you choose third person omniscient?

Choose third person omniscient when:

- **Your story spans a large cast, a long timeline or a whole society.** The narrator can cover ground that no single character could.
- **You want dramatic irony.** The reader knows more than the characters, and that gap creates tension.
- **A narrator's voice is part of the appeal.** Humour, authority and wisdom can belong to the narrator.
- **Your world needs explaining in the telling.** Epic fantasy and historical fiction often benefit from a narrator who can step back and describe, though you still need to avoid dumping [exposition](/blog/what-is-exposition/).

Omniscient narration asks more of you. Aim to keep the narrator's voice interesting and the transitions clear, and show events through scene and action.

## How do you decide between limited and omniscient?

Work through four questions:

1. **How many characters carry the story?** One to three points to limited. A whole community points to omniscient or a rotating limited cast.
2. **How close should the reader feel?** Close to thought and feeling favours limited. Perspective and scope favour omniscient.
3. **Does the reader need to know more than the characters?** If so, omniscient delivers it directly. Limited can still do it by switching characters.
4. **Does the story want a narrator with a personality?** If yes, omniscient gives that voice room.

Then test your answer. Write the same key scene in limited and in omniscient and compare which one gives the effect you want.

> **Writing Desk tip:** Use chapter variants to test both options. Branch the chapter, rewrite the scene in the other point of view, then compare the two side by side. If the new version does not work, roll back to the original.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between third person limited and omniscient?

Limited stays inside one character's perspective and reports only what that character perceives. Omniscient has a narrator who knows everything, including every character's thoughts and events no one witnesses.

### What is third person omniscient?

Third person omniscient is narration by an outside narrator with unlimited knowledge of the characters, events and world. The narrator can move between minds, comment on the story and reveal what characters do not know.

### What is deep third person?

Deep third is the closest form of third person limited. The narrator's voice merges with the character's, so thoughts and perceptions come through directly without tags such as *she thought*.

### What is head-hopping?

Head-hopping is moving between characters' thoughts within a scene without a signal to the reader. It confuses viewpoint, and the fix is one point of view per scene or a deliberate omniscient narrator.

### Can third person limited have multiple point of view characters?

Yes. Many novels rotate between several characters, with each scene or chapter held to one viewpoint. *A Game of Thrones* labels each chapter with its point-of-view character.

### Which is better for fantasy, limited or omniscient?

Both work. Epic fantasy often uses rotating limited narrators for intimacy across a large cast, and omniscient narration for a story-teller's voice and breadth. Pick the one that fits how you want the reader to meet your world.

### Is third person omniscient hard to write?

It asks for a controlled narrator and clear transitions between minds. Writers who find it difficult can start with limited narration and widen the distance on purpose.

## Final words

Pick the camera your story needs. Limited gives you closeness and a reader who learns alongside the character. Omniscient gives you scope, irony and a narrator with a voice. Whichever you choose, hold it with intent and move deliberately when you need to change.
