An unreliable narrator is a point-of-view character whose account of events the reader can't fully trust, whether because they're lying, mistaken, limited in what they understand, or psychologically compromised in some way that distorts what they perceive.

The technique works because most fiction trains readers to trust the narrator by default. A story told in first person, or in close third person aligned tightly with one character's perspective, usually asks the reader to accept that character's version of events as the truth of the story. An unreliable narrator breaks that contract deliberately, and the gap between what the narrator believes and what's actually true becomes part of what the reader is piecing together.

## Types of Unreliable Narrator

**The deliberate liar.** The narrator knows the truth and is actively concealing or distorting it from the reader, often to protect themselves or manipulate how they're perceived.

**The self-deceived narrator.** The narrator isn't lying to the reader so much as lying to themselves, and the reader gradually sees through a version of events the narrator genuinely believes.

**The limited or naive narrator.** A child narrator, or one who simply lacks the knowledge or context to fully understand the events they're describing, creating a gap the reader can see but the narrator can't.

**The psychologically compromised narrator.** Trauma, mental illness, substance use, or memory impairment affects what the narrator perceives or remembers, and the reader has to account for that distortion when interpreting events.

## Why It Works

An unreliable narrator turns reading into active interpretation rather than passive reception. The reader is doing two jobs at once, following the story the narrator is telling and independently reconstructing what actually happened, and the tension between those two versions is often more compelling than either version alone would be.

## How to Signal Unreliability Without Losing the Reader

**Plant contradictions.** Small inconsistencies between what the narrator claims and what other characters say or do give an attentive reader something to notice, without requiring the narrator to announce their own unreliability.

**Use other characters' reactions.** If people in the story consistently respond to the narrator in ways that don't match the narrator's account of themselves, that gap becomes a signal.

**Be consistent with the type of unreliability you've chosen.** A narrator who's self-deceived should be consistently self-deceived in a recognisable pattern; a narrator who's actively lying should show consistent gaps in what they choose to reveal. Randomly unreliable narration, with no internal logic to the distortion, reads as authorial inconsistency rather than a deliberate technique.

**Reward attentive readers before the reveal, not only at it.** A twist built entirely on unreliability that's had no visible groundwork tends to feel less like a revelation and more like the rules changing after the fact.

## Final Words

An unreliable narrator isn't a trick to spring on the reader at the end. It's a sustained relationship between what's told and what's true, held consistently across the whole novel. The most memorable examples aren't the ones where the reader is simply fooled; they're the ones where, looking back, every clue was there the whole time.
