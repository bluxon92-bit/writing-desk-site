An adverb modifying a verb is a sentence telling on itself. *She walked slowly* is a verb, *walked*, that wasn't specific enough, propped up by a word explaining what it should have shown. The fix is finding the verb that didn't need one.

## The Core Problem

**Weak verb, adverb prop:** She walked slowly across the room.
**Stronger verb, no prop:** She shuffled across the room.

**Weak verb, adverb prop:** He said angrily.
**Stronger verb, no prop:** He snapped.

**Weak verb, adverb prop:** The door closed loudly.
**Stronger verb, no prop:** The door slammed.

In each case, the adverb was compensating for a verb that could have carried the specific meaning on its own. *Shuffled* already contains *slowly*. *Snapped* already contains *angrily*. *Slammed* already contains *loudly*. Once the stronger verb is doing the work, the adverb becomes redundant weight sitting on top of a sentence that didn't need it.

This is the pattern worth watching for specifically: an adverb next to a generic verb, *walked, said, moved, looked, went*, is a sign there's a more precise verb one step away.

## Dialogue Tags Are the Worst Offenders

**He said angrily.** **She said sadly.** **"I don't care," he said dismissively.**

Dialogue tag adverbs are the most common version of this problem, and the easiest to fix once you're looking for it. Either the dialogue itself should be doing the emotional work, in which case the adverb is redundant, or the tag needs a stronger verb, or the surrounding action needs to carry it instead.

**Redundant:** "I don't care," he said dismissively.
**Trust the dialogue:** "I don't care."

**Stronger verb:** "I don't care," he snapped.

**Action carries it:** He didn't look up. "I don't care."

Three different fixes for the same underlying problem, and none of them keep the adverb. If the dialogue and the context around it aren't already conveying the emotion, adding an adverb to the tag is rarely enough to fix that on its own, it's patching a gap that needs a different kind of repair.

## When to Keep Them

Cutting every adverb from a manuscript is its own kind of mistake. Some are doing real work.

**No available replacement verb.** Some meanings don't have a single-word verb equivalent. *She spoke quietly* doesn't have an obvious one-word substitute that means exactly the same thing without sounding strange. *Murmured* is close but not identical. Sometimes the adverb is the clearest option available.

**Precision the verb alone can't carry.** *He nodded slowly* and *he nodded quickly* mean different things, and there isn't a single verb for each. The adverb is adding real information, not padding.

**Rhythm.** Occasionally a sentence just reads better with the adverb in it, and craft rules exist to serve the sentence, not the other way round. If cutting an adverb makes a sentence worse, the sentence wins.

**Adverbs that aren't modifying manner.** *Already, still, almost, barely, only* are adverbs too, and they're doing structural or temporal work rather than propping up a weak verb. This article is about the *-ly* manner adverbs stacked next to verbs and dialogue tags specifically, not adverbs as a category.

The test that matters: does the sentence lose real information if the adverb is cut, or does it just lose a word. If it loses information, keep it. If it loses nothing, it wasn't doing anything.

## How to Find Them

Search for *-ly* across a chapter, most manner adverbs end that way, and check each one against the test above. Pay particular attention to dialogue tags, since that's where the pattern concentrates most heavily.

> **Writing Desk tip:** The AI Editor's line edit lens flags adverbs propping up weak verbs as part of its chapter-by-chapter feedback, alongside filter words and passive constructions, so the pattern surfaces without a manual search through every *-ly* word in the manuscript.
