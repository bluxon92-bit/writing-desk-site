A flashback is a scene that interrupts the present-day story to dramatise something that happened earlier, taking the reader fully into a past event rather than alluding to it through [backstory](/blog/backstory/) delivered in the present tense.

Flashbacks have a poor reputation in some writing circles, largely because they're so often used badly: dropped in as an information dump disguised as a scene, stopping the present-day story's momentum to explain something the reader could have learned more efficiently another way. Used well, though, a flashback does something a paragraph of [backstory](/blog/backstory/) can't. It lets the reader experience a past event directly, with its own tension and stakes, rather than being told about it after the fact.

## When a Flashback Earns Its Place

**When the past event has its own dramatic tension**, not just informational value. A flashback that's essentially just a character remembering a fact reads as a detour. A flashback with genuine stakes, conflict, a scene that could stand on its own, reads as a scene.

**When the timing recontextualises something in the present.** A flashback placed right after a present-day moment that doesn't make sense on its own can land as a genuine revelation rather than an interruption, because the reader arrives at it already wanting the answer.

**When it can't be conveyed as effectively any other way.** If the same information could land through a line of dialogue, an object, a single memory-triggered image, those options are almost always less disruptive to [pacing](/blog/pacing/) than a full scene set in the past.

## Common Mistakes

**Flashbacks that are longer than the tension they're built on can support.** A flashback that runs several chapters risks losing the reader's connection to the present-day story entirely, at which point the "interruption" has effectively become a second story.

**Too many, too early.** A flashback in the first chapter, before the reader has any investment in the present-day story, has nothing to interrupt yet and nothing to recontextualise.

**Unclear transitions.** Readers need to know immediately that they've moved in time. Ambiguous transitions that leave the reader disoriented about when a scene is taking place cost more than they gain, however elegant they seem on the sentence level.

## An Alternative Worth Considering

Before reaching for a flashback, ask whether the information could be delivered through implication in the present instead: a character's physical reaction, a detail they avoid mentioning, a choice that only makes sense in light of something unstated. Often the version of a story that trusts the reader to feel the weight of an unexplained past is stronger than the version that stops to explain it directly.

## Final Words

A flashback isn't a shortcut around [exposition](/blog/what-is-exposition/); done badly, it's exposition with extra steps. Done well, it's a scene in its own right, with its own stakes, placed exactly where its impact on the present-day story is highest. If a flashback wouldn't work as a standalone scene, it's worth asking whether it belongs in the manuscript at all.
