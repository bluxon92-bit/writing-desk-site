The advice "start with action" gets misused more than almost any other piece of writing guidance. Writers take it to mean open with a chase, a fight, an explosion, something kinetic, and are then baffled when feedback comes back saying the opening doesn't work. The reader doesn't care yet.

*In medias res* is not "start with action." It's something more precise, more interesting, and more difficult: start in the middle of something that already matters. The difference is everything.

---

## What It Actually Means

The phrase is Latin, Horace used it in *Ars Poetica*, describing how Homer begins the *Iliad* not at the start of the Trojan War but years into it, at a moment of crisis within the conflict. The war's causes, the years that preceded this moment, the context: all of it arrives later, as the story earns it.

*In medias res* means beginning your story at a dramatically significant moment rather than at the chronological beginning. The story is already in motion when the reader arrives.

What makes an opening *in medias res* rather than simply fast: the moment you open on has weight. The reader doesn't yet know why it matters, but they feel that it does. The stakes are present, even if they're not yet explained.

---

## Why It Works

When you open in medias res, two things happen simultaneously. The reader is pulled forward, there's momentum from the first line, because something is happening. And they're simultaneously pulled back, they want to understand the context, the history, the relationship between what they're seeing and everything that preceded it.

That dual pull, forward and backward at the same time, is one of the most powerful reading experiences available to a novelist. The reader is asking "what happens next?" and "how did we get here?" at once. Both questions have to be answered, and answering them through the story rather than through pre-story setup creates a kind of sustained tension that a conventional beginning struggles to match.

---

## The Three Ways to Do It

**Open at the inciting incident**

The most common version. Your story begins at the moment the protagonist's ordinary world is disrupted, the arrival of the thing that will drive the rest of the novel. There's no ordinary world section to establish, no gradual introduction to character and setting. We arrive at the disruption directly.

This works best when the reader can orient themselves quickly, when the genre conventions, the prose voice, or the specificity of the moment gives them enough to stand on while the context arrives.

**Open after the inciting incident**

You begin in the middle of the fallout. Something has already happened; the story opens in its aftermath. The *what happened* becomes a question the reader wants answered, and you reveal it gradually as the forward story develops.

*Gone Girl* opens after something has clearly gone wrong. *Everything I Never Told You* opens with a death. The reader is oriented not in the protagonist's ordinary world but in the wreckage of an event, and the novel works to explain how it happened.

**Open at the climax, then circle back**

The most audacious version: the story opens at or near its own ending, then cuts to the beginning of the story that led there. The reader knows where things are going. The question is how.

This structure creates irony, the reader watches events unfold with knowledge the characters don't have. Depending on your story, that's either unbearable tension or it's distance; know which one your novel produces before you commit to it.

---

## What It Is Not

**It is not just starting with action.** An opening car chase in which the reader has no investment in the driver is not in medias res, it's a [prologue](/blog/what-is-a-prologue/) waiting for a story to attach to. The action has to carry weight, and weight requires that the reader cares about something. The character, the stakes, the world, at least one of these has to be legible from the first page.

**It is not an excuse to withhold context indefinitely.** The orientation that a conventional opening provides, who is this person, what do they want, what world do they inhabit, still has to arrive. In medias res delays it; it doesn't eliminate it. If the reader is disoriented past the point of curiosity and into the territory of frustration, you've waited too long.

**It is not the only way to write a strong opening.** The quiet, atmospheric first chapter that earns its tension gradually is not a lesser technique. It's a different one. The choice between them should come from the story, not from received wisdom about what a first chapter is supposed to do.

---

## When to Use It

**Your story has an irresistible opening moment.** If the most dramatically compelling scene in your novel is not the beginning of its chronology, begin there. Work backwards or around it. The chronological start of your story is not necessarily the right start for your novel.

**Your genre demands immediate engagement.** Thriller, crime, horror, certain YA, these genres have reader expectations baked in. A slow atmospheric opening that works beautifully in literary fiction may read as evasion in a thriller.

**Your protagonist reveals themselves better under pressure than in calm.** Some characters are most alive when things are going wrong. Dropping them into crisis on page one, rather than introducing them in their ordinary life and waiting for the crisis to arrive, lets them be fully themselves from the start.

**The [backstory](/blog/backstory/) is richer than the setup.** If what happened before your story begins is more interesting than the forward plot in its early stages, in medias res lets you tell both simultaneously rather than sequencing them.

---

## The Risk

The risk is real: a reader who can't orient themselves quickly enough will not keep reading. In medias res raises the bar for your prose, your voice, and your first chapter's ability to carry the reader through confusion into curiosity.

If you're going to begin in the middle, make the middle irresistible. Make the first sentence do work. Make the first page create a question the reader cannot walk away from. The conventional opening gives you several chapters to earn the reader's investment. In medias res asks for it immediately.

---

## In Medias Res in the Context of Structure

Starting in medias res doesn't replace structural thinking, it changes where your structural beats land relative to your story's chronology. If you open at the inciting incident, your Act One is compressed. If you open after it, your Act One is largely retrospective. If you open at the climax, you're writing the entire novel as backstory to a moment the reader has already seen.

Whatever structure you're working with, three-act, [Hero's Journey](/blog/heros-journey/), Fichtean Curve, the beats still need to be there. In medias res is an opening strategy, not a structural framework. The two work together.
