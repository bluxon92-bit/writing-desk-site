Editing your novel, the complete guide

We take a look at how to go about self-editing your novel. Story structure and characters first, then sentence-level craft, then the technical pass. 

Overview The three passes AI editorial feedback Go deeper Get the checklist

One of the mistakes I made when I eventually got to the business of editing my first book was approaching self-editing without a structure.

I simply read through the manuscript, editing as I went. this meant fixing obvious mistakes, polishing those clumsy sentences, adding in details here and there. Took a long time and when I got to the end I realised large sections probably needed a full rewrite (or actually to be cut), and all the tweaks and polish I'd just spent hours on were wasted effort.

This did not make it easier to kill my darlings.

The answer I eventually came to, was to split self-editing into three distinct passes. First a structural pass, then a line edit, proof-reading pass. This guide explains what each pass is best at, the specific problems worth hunting for in each, and where structured feedback (AI or otherwise) can stand in as a second pair of eyes before the manuscript goes any further.

The three self-editing passes

You've written a book, well done. But a first draft is a messy thing and you're not at the finishing line next. This part, the editing stage is probably my least favourite part of writing a book, and also the part you absolutely have to get right.

Now, before you go any further, before you reread, or touch a line, just pause. Put your manuscript away for as long as you can bare. Work on another project for a bit, and then come back with fresh eyes.

  1. 1

    Structural pass

    Does every scene earn its place? Does the pacing hold? Is the point of view consistent? Do each of your characters have arcs?

    Everything downstream depends on the structure being settled first which is why we just told you not to jump straight in. You need to be able to look at your work honestly, without bias. You need to be able to say to yourself that the beautifully written scene in the middle of the book doesn't advance the plot, doesn't reveal important information, that it needs to just be cut completely.

    This editing phase is all about determining whether or not you've pulled off the big picture. Whole scenes will get cut or rewritten. You need to be ruthless. Strategic. And bold.

    • How to Self-Edit Your Novel — the full pass order, including what to check for in the structural read
  2. 2

    Line pass

    The next phase is about making your prose sing. This is where you go through your work, paragraph by paragraph, sentence by sentence, word by word. Look at rhythm, word choice, dialogue, passive voice, showing vs telling, filter words, weak words, and adverbs.

    Avoid the temptation to overwrite, you're not aiming for flourish, you're aiming for clarity and pacing. The language should get out of the way of the story. Your sentences should carry the reader inexorably forward.

    • Filter Words in Fiction — what they are and how to cut them
    • Passive Voice in Fiction — when it's the right choice, and when it's just habit
    • Weak Words in Fiction — the full list to search your draft for
    • Adverbs in Fiction — the specific pattern that signals a weak verb underneath
  3. 3

    Technical pass

    The final phase is about polish. Grammar, spelling, formatting. You should also look at place names, eye-colour, all those little details that need to be consistent throughout the story lest you break the readers immersion.

    You'll naturally catch a lot of this whilst you're writing and redrafting. But, try and avoid focusing on polish until the previous two passes are complete.

AI editorial feedback

One of the major problems with self-editing is page-blindness. Even if you leave your manuscript for six months before coming back to edit it, you know what you're trying to say, it's written in your voice, and so it can be incredibly hard to spot mistakes, clumsy sentences, and even sometimes glaring plot holes. You're too close to tell whether you've been wonderfully subtle with your information reveals, heavy-handed, or didn't actually reveal the information at all.

We've built into Writing Desk an AI editor to help solve exactly this problem. It reads your chapter, and gives structured, honest feedback across four editorial lenses. This is not a replacement for human feedback. Rather a quick way to get a fresh perspective on your work.

Developmental

Structure, pacing, and whether the chapter is doing its job within the wider story.

Beta reader

How the chapter lands for a reader coming to it fresh: engaging, confusing, or somewhere between.

Line edit

Filter words, passive constructions, adverbs, and rhythm, applied consistently across every chapter.

World editor

Cross-referenced against your own resource sheets, so details don't quietly drift between chapters.

For each chapter, it highlights specific strengths and weaknesses rather than a generic score, so you know what to look at instead of just knowing something's off. A chat function lets you interrogate any note further, ask why it was flagged, or argue your case. Act on the feedback or ignore it, it's up to you.

Our AI editor will never won't do is write anything for you. It never generates or touches your prose, every suggestion is yours to take or leave. Think of it as a second pair of eyes for moments where you don't have a beta reader to hand.

Knowing when to stop

Self-editing ends when you've stopped finding problems you can see yourself.

That's a different milestone from the manuscript being finished. It's the edge of what self-editing alone can do, structural and technical, line by line, pass by pass.

What's left past that edge needs eyes that aren't yours, a beta reader, an AI pass, or eventually a developmental editor.
