How to write a novel, start to finish

The complete process, laid out step by step: finding the idea, knowing your characters, planning enough to start, drafting, surviving the middle, and revising, with a deeper guide linked at every stage.

Overview The seven steps Craft fundamentals A note on timeline Plan it in Writing Desk

The first novel finished by most writers takes years, not because it's long, but because there's no process yet. Ambition and enthusiasm, and no real idea what comes next. Starting, stalling, restarting from a different angle, losing months to drafts that go nowhere.

The second one is faster. Same complexity, same genre, better process.

This is that process, laid out step by step, with a deeper guide linked wherever one exists.

The seven steps

Not a rigid formula, a sequence that holds up across genres and gets easier to trust the more times you use it.

  1. 1

    Find the idea worth 80,000 words

    Every idea feels exciting at first. Most aren't exciting enough to survive the middle of a novel. Push it: what's the emotional core, not the plot, the feeling? Can you say it in one sentence, not a synopsis, a hook? A working title helps too, even a bad one, it gives the idea a handle to refer back to while it's still forming.

    • Book Title Generator — shake loose some options if nothing's coming
  2. 2

    Know your characters before you start

    You don't need to know everything about a protagonist before writing the first chapter. But you need to know three things: what they want, what they're afraid of, and what they're wrong about. That last one is where character arcs come from.

    • Character Arc: The Complete Guide for Novelists — how that wrongness turns into transformation
    • How to Create a Character Profile — the full template for building a character before drafting
    • Character Motivation — the want/need distinction that drives every scene
  3. 3

    Plan enough to start writing

    You don't have to outline in detail. But you need to know where you're going, specifically how the story ends, and what the major turning points are that get you there. At minimum: the protagonist's problem and goal, the inciting incident, the midpoint shift, and the ending.

    • How to Write a Novel Outline — five different outlining methods and how to pick one
    • The Snowflake Method — worth a look if "outline the book" feels paralysing
    • Story Structure hub — every major framework if you want more scaffolding than a bullet list
  4. 4

    Write the first draft badly

    The first draft is not the book. It's the raw material that gets shaped into the book, and the fear of writing badly stops more novels than anything else. Set a daily word count and protect it. Don't edit as you go, forward momentum is everything in a first draft.

    • How to Write Faster — the specific habits that actually increase output
    • Writer's Block — read the moment momentum stalls, since the fix depends on which of five causes is happening
  5. 5

    Get through the middle

    The middle is where novels go to die. The problem is usually one of two things: nothing is getting worse, or nothing is changing. In a good second act, the protagonist's situation should escalate, each complication narrowing their options.

    • Four-Act Structure — fixes a sagging middle by giving the midpoint a genuine act break
    • Pacing — proportion and momentum at every level, sentence to structure
  6. 6

    Write an ending that earns it

    An ending doesn't have to be happy, and it doesn't have to resolve everything. But it has to feel earned, meaning the outcome grows from the choices the protagonist made. If the climax depends on coincidence or rescue, it falls flat no matter how dramatic the scene is.

    • What Is a Deus Ex Machina? — names exactly this failure mode and how to build around it
    • Character Arc — what makes a climax feel like the only place the story could have gone
  7. 7

    Let it rest, then revise

    Finish the draft. Put it away for at least two weeks, ideally longer. Come back and read it like a reader, not a writer, diagnosing structural problems before touching a single sentence.

    • Revising Your First Draft — the full pass order, and why doing it in any other order wastes real time
A note on the timeline

Nobody writes a great novel on the first attempt.

Four years for a first book is common. Eight to eleven months for a second, with the same complexity, isn't unusual either. The improvement isn't talent, it's process.

Almost nobody gets it right first time. But a finished one is entirely possible, and that's where everything starts.
