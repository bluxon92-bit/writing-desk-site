Short stories from the golden age of science fiction

Between roughly 1935 and the early 1960s, a generation of writers worked out almost everything science fiction would go on to be — first contact, robot ethics, simulated reality, surveillance dystopia, faster-than-light travel, the unthinkable. And they did it in a few thousand words at a time.

This collection gathers ten of the cleanest, sharpest examples in the public domain — not just to read, but to study. Notice where each writer opens the scene, how long they wait to reveal the twist, and how little they explain. Youth

by Isaac Asimov

Two boys find strange creatures in their garden and decide to keep them. The creatures, it turns out, are doing the same. A quietly perfect first-contact story with a twist Asimov throws in effortlessly.

Craft noteWatch how long Asimov withholds the reveal — and how little he needs to spell it out.

Let's Get Together

by Isaac Asimov

Cold War paranoia at full stretch: ten humanoid robots are smuggled into the United States — each carrying a fragment of a hydrogen bomb that will only arm when they all stand in the same room.

Craft noteA masterclass in raising stakes through a simple countdown mechanic, not exposition.

The Tunnel Under the World

by Frederik Pohl

Guy Birckhead wakes from a dream of dying in an explosion. The same advertisement plays on every channel. The town is not the town it appears — and neither, possibly, is he.

by Paul W. Fairman

A physicist's equation could end the world — or save it. The trouble is no one, the physicist included, can yet tell which side of the line his proof actually falls on.

Doorstep

by Keith Laumer

An enormous craft has landed on the White House lawn and refuses to open. A government negotiator is sent in. The story is short, sly, and almost entirely about interacting with what is in front of you.

Craft noteOne location, one problem, no subplots — a lesson in restraint and scene economy.

by Jules & Michel Verne

A media baron's ordinary workday, one thousand years from now — flying machines, video calls, advertising projected onto clouds. Remarkable for how much he got right.
